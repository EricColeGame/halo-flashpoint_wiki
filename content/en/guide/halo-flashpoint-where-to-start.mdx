## What Is Halo Flashpoint, and Why It's a Great First Skirmish Game

Halo Flashpoint is a tabletop miniatures skirmish game published by Mantic Games under license from the Halo franchise. Instead of commanding vast armies, you control a small squad — a fireteam of Spartans, a Covenant strike group, or a mixed force — and fight over objectives on a compact battlefield.

If you're coming from the video games, the appeal is obvious: the factions, weapons, and heroes you already recognize, translated into a physical battle you can see from above. If you're coming from other miniatures games, the appeal is that it's a genuinely small commitment. A handful of models, a box of terrain, and a rulebook you can read in an evening.

| Detail | What to Expect |
| --- | --- |
| Publisher | Mantic Games, under license from the Halo rights holders |
| Setting | The Halo universe — UNSC forces against the Covenant |
| Game Type | Tabletop miniatures skirmish game |
| Force Size | A small squad per side, often well under a dozen models |
| Typical Play Time | Roughly an hour for a standard scenario once you know the rules |
| What You Need to Start | One core starter set; everything beyond that is optional |

Why that matters for beginners:

- **Low model count.** You're painting and assembling a squad, not a battalion.
- **Short games.** An evening is enough for two or three matches.
- **Self-contained boxes.** The starter sets are built to be played straight out of the box.
- **Familiar factions.** You already have an emotional attachment to the models before you roll a single die.

The game leans on the things skirmish players expect: alternating model activations, objective-based scenarios, and a dice pool where you roll a handful of dice and count successes against a target's stats. If you've played other small-scale games like Kill Team or Star Wars: Shatterpoint, the rhythm will feel familiar.

## Your First Decision: Choosing a Starter Set

The single most important purchase you'll make is your first box, and it's also where most beginners overthink things. At launch, Mantic released two core starter boxes, and either one is a valid answer to the Halo Flashpoint where to start question.

| Starter Box | Theme | Best For | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spartan Edition | A UNSC fireteam built around Spartans | Players who want to play the heroes of the games | Community reports often call this the more popular entry point |
| Elite Edition | A Covenant force led by Elites | Players who prefer alien factions or want a rival squad | Pairs naturally with a Spartan box |
| Two Opposite Boxes | One of each faction | Two friends starting together | The most cost-effective route to a full table for two players |

If a friend wants to play too, the smartest move is for each of you to buy the opposite box. You end up with two complete factions, more terrain than a single box provides, and a permanent rival to test lists against.

Here's how to choose in about thirty seconds:

- Love playing Spartans in the games? **Spartan Edition.**
- Prefer the Covenant? **Elite Edition.**
- Starting with a friend? **Buy opposite boxes.**
- Genuinely can't decide? **Pick the models you'd enjoy painting**, because you'll spend more time with them than with any rule.

A quick note on "which box is stronger": neither is designed to dominate. Community reports consistently describe the two starter factions as closely matched, and the deciding factor is almost always which models you're excited to put on the table.

## The Halo Flashpoint Where to Start Checklist

Before you buy anything else, understand this: the starter set is designed to be playable out of the box. You don't need to paint anything, and you don't need a dedicated gaming table. Everything below the first row is a convenience, not a requirement.

| Item | Why You Need It | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core starter set | Rules, models, dice, tokens, and terrain in one purchase | Essential |
| Flush cutters | Removes parts from sprues without damaging them | Essential |
| Plastic cement | Assembles models permanently and cleanly | Soon |
| Hobby knife | Cleans mold lines and small imperfections | Soon |
| Primer spray | Gives paint something to grip | Optional at first |
| Paints and brushes | Makes your squad recognizably yours | Optional at first |
| Storage box with foam or magnets | Protects models in transit | Soon |
| Play mat or board | Defines the play area and looks great | Nice to have |

The two hobby purchases worth making early are a decent pair of flush cutters and a bottle of plastic cement. Buy quality once instead of replacing cheap tools three times. Everything else can wait until you've played a few games and know you're sticking with it.

Things you can safely skip for now: an airbrush, a wet palette, premium terrain kits, and a dedicated figure case. Those are rewards for later, not entry fees.

## Learning the Rules: A Realistic First-Week Plan

The biggest mistake new players make is trying to learn everything before playing a single game. Rules only stick once you've actually used them. If you're still figuring out Halo Flashpoint where to start, this schedule is the fastest route from unopened box to confident player.

| Day | Focus | Time Needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Read the core rules once, cover to cover, without trying to memorize | 45 minutes |
| 2 | Assemble the models from your starter set | 1–2 hours |
| 3 | Play a stripped-down game: movement, shooting, melee, no objectives | 45 minutes |
| 4 | Add a scenario with objective markers | 1 hour |
| 5 | Re-read only the rules you had to look up mid-game | 20 minutes |
| 6 | Build a second force list with a different mix of units | 30 minutes |
| 7 | Play again, ideally against a different opponent | 1 hour |

A few habits that separate players who stick with the hobby from those who don't:

- **Play before you feel ready.** Nobody's first game is clean.
- **Start with less terrain.** Open boards teach movement and line of sight; dense boards teach frustration.
- **Play the objectives.** Fighting in the center is fun, but scenario play is where the game's depth lives.
- **Don't buy more boxes until you've played three games.** Your first purchase instincts are usually wrong.

## Expanding Your Force and Finding Opponents

Once you've played a handful of games, the temptation is to buy everything at once. Resist it for a month. Skirmish games reward depth over breadth, and a well-painted squad you understand beats a shelf of unopened boxes every time.

| Priority | What to Add | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A second squad for the opposing faction | You need two forces to play with a friend |
| 2 | More terrain | Terrain changes games more than new models do |
| 3 | Unit expansions that fill a gap in your list | Fixes weaknesses you noticed in your first games |
| 4 | A completely different faction | Keeps the game fresh over the long term |

When you do expand, think in terms of roles rather than individual models. Most skirmish forces break down into a few jobs, and Halo Flashpoint follows that pattern closely.

| Role | In Halo Terms | Job on the Table |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Line Trooper | Marines, Grunts | Cheap bodies that hold objectives and absorb fire |
| Leader | Spartans, Elites | Powerful and flexible — usually your best single model |
| Support | Heavy weapons, specialists | Breaks stalemates and handles tough targets |
| Skirmisher | Jackals, scouts | Fast, disruptive, grabs objectives early |

Finding opponents is easier than most people expect. Local game stores often host open hobby nights, and tabletop clubs are usually delighted to teach a new player. Online communities built around Mantic's games and the wider skirmish scene are another reliable route, and many of them maintain opponent-finding threads. If none of that works, teaching a friend costs you nothing but a spare squad.

For the official miniature range, rule updates, and product announcements, the best single destination is [Mantic Games' official store and news hub](https://www.manticgames.com/), which lists the current Halo Flashpoint line and downloadable resources.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Halo Flashpoint

**Do I need two starter sets to play Halo Flashpoint?**
No. One core starter set contains everything two players need for a complete game, including models for both sides, dice, and terrain. A second box only makes sense if you want a permanent second faction or a larger battlefield.

**Do I have to paint the miniatures before playing?**
Not at all. Unpainted models play exactly the same as painted ones, and many players spend their first few months playing bare plastic while they build confidence with a brush.

**How long does a typical game take?**
Most standard scenarios run about an hour once both players know the basics. Your first two games will take longer because of constant rulebook checks — that's normal and not a sign you're doing it wrong.

**What's the best answer to "Halo Flashpoint where to start" if I've never played a miniatures game before?**
Keep it simple. Buy one starter set, assemble it over a weekend, and play a stripped-down game with no objectives before you add anything else. The rules will make far more sense once you've moved models around a table, and you'll learn what you actually want to buy next instead of guessing.

The real answer to "Halo Flashpoint where to start" is smaller than most people expect: one box, one afternoon, one opponent. Everything after that is just deciding how deep you want to go.
