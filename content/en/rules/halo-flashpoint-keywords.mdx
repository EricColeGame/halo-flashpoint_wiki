## Why the Halo Flashpoint Keywords on Your Cards Decide the Game

Halo: Flashpoint packs a full skirmish battle into a handful of miniatures, but the game is often decided in the quiet two minutes before anyone rolls a die — the moment you read your cards. Every profile, weapon, and mission is written in a shared shorthand, and a player who understands that shorthand will beat a player with a prettier roster almost every time. Learning the core Halo Flashpoint keywords is the fastest way to go from "I think I can shoot" to "I know exactly what happens next." The good news is that the vocabulary is small and consistent, so once the Halo Flashpoint keywords click, you can read a brand-new unit card in seconds instead of minutes.

This guide walks through the terms you'll meet in your first few games, groups them by the part of the turn they belong to, and flags the ones that cause the most table arguments. Treat it as a translation layer between the rulebook and the table.

## Start Here: The Halo Flashpoint Keywords That Appear on Every Card

Halo: Flashpoint is a skirmish game, which means you command a small force — a fireteam rather than an army — and every single model matters. That design choice shapes the vocabulary: instead of rules for regiments and formations, you get tight, per-model terms that repeat across every faction. [Mantic Games](https://www.manticgames.com/) publishes the game, and the rulebook keeps its terminology deliberately lean so a newcomer can learn the basics in one sitting.

Here's the short list you'll meet in your first ten minutes.

| Keyword | Plain-English meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Activate | Choosing a model (or unit) and resolving its actions this turn | Almost every tactical decision flows from activation order |
| Action | The individual things a model can do when activated — move, shoot, fight, interact | Your action budget is the real currency of a turn |
| Move | A measured distance a model travels across the table | Movement is what turns a good plan into a bad one for your opponent |
| Shoot | A ranged attack against a target you can see | The default way to remove enemy models at distance |
| Melee | A close-quarters attack against a model in base contact | Often the only reliable way to finish off a tough target |
| Wound | A measure of how much damage a model can absorb before it's removed | Wounds, not kills, tell you who is actually winning |
| Save / Armor | A roll or modifier that can cancel incoming damage | Explains why some models shrug off shots that drop others |
| Cover | Protection granted by terrain between attacker and target | The single biggest modifier in most firefights |
| Line of Sight | Whether an attacker can actually see its target | The gate that every ranged attack passes through first |
| Objective | A mission-specific thing you must control, hold, or reach | Objectives, not kills, decide most games |
| Deployment Zone | The area where your force starts the game | Deployment mistakes are very hard to undo |
| Points Cost | The budget value of a model or upgrade | The number that keeps two rosters fair |
| Unit Keyword / Ability | A named rule printed on a card that overrides the basics | This is where factions start to feel different from one another |

Card wording can shift slightly between factions and expansions, so treat this as a map rather than a substitute for the rulebook itself.

## Turn Structure Keywords: Activation, Initiative, and Reactions

Most skirmish games of this style use alternating activation: you activate one model, your opponent activates one model, and you keep trading until both forces are done. That rhythm is the heartbeat of the game, and the keywords around it tell you who moves when.

The reason this matters is tempo. If you spend your best model's activation early, your opponent gets to react with a fully informed plan. If you hold it back, you may lose the chance to use it at all. Community reports from early events consistently describe activation order as the single biggest skill gap between new and experienced players — not dice luck.

| Stage | Keyword you'll see | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Deploy / Deployment Zone | Both players place their forces inside their own zone, usually alternating placements |
| Initiative | Initiative / First Activation | You determine who takes the first activation of the round |
| Unit turns | Activate / Activation | Players alternate activating one model or unit at a time |
| Actions | Move, Shoot, Melee, Interact | The activated model spends its limited actions |
| Reactions | React / Response | Certain units can act out of sequence when a trigger occurs |
| Round end | End Phase / Scoring | Objectives are checked, effects expire, and the next round begins |

**Practical tip:** before you activate anything, say out loud which two models you plan to activate last. If you can't name them, you don't have a plan yet.

## Combat Keywords: Dice Pools, Cover, and Line of Sight

Nearly every attack in the game follows the same shape: check line of sight, build a dice pool, roll, count successes, then let the defender try to cancel the damage. Once you internalize that sequence, the combat section of the rulebook stops looking like a wall of text.

| Keyword | What it governs | Practical tip |
|---|---|---|
| Line of Sight | Whether a shot is legal at all | Check this before you measure anything else |
| Cover | A defensive bonus granted by terrain | Denying cover is often worth more than a bigger gun |
| Dice Pool | How many dice you roll for an attack | More dice beats better dice more often than players expect |
| Target Number | The value a die needs to score a success | Know it before you roll so you don't slow the table down |
| Success | A die that met the target number | Count successes out loud — it keeps the game honest |
| Damage / Wound Roll | Whether a hit actually hurts | High-damage weapons are wasted on cheap targets |
| Save / Armor | Whether the target shrugs the hit off | Armor should change your target priority entirely |

Two habits separate clean games from messy ones. First, always resolve line of sight before range — arguing about distance when the shot was never legal is the most common time-waster in the hobby. Second, announce your modifiers before rolling. In player experience, most disputes aren't about the rules themselves but about someone quietly adding a cover bonus after the dice have landed.

## Mission Keywords: How You Actually Win a Game

Here's the trap that catches nearly every new skirmish player: the game looks like a shooting gallery, but it's scored like a race. Mission keywords define what the table actually wants from you, and they change from scenario to scenario.

| Objective type | Keyword you'll see | What it asks of you |
|---|---|---|
| Hold | Control / Hold | Keep a model alive and in place for a set number of rounds |
| Capture | Secure / Interact | Move onto an objective and spend an action to claim it |
| Eliminate | Bounty / Target | Remove a specific enemy model from the table |
| Retrieve | Carry / Extract | Pick up an item and get it back to safety |
| Survive | Endgame / Last Stand | Keep enough of your force alive until the final round |

**Actionable tip:** write the mission's scoring keywords at the top of your notes before deployment. Then deploy *for that mission* rather than for a generic firefight. A roster built to hold ground will lose badly in an extraction scenario, and vice versa.

## Five Mistakes Players Make with Halo Flashpoint Keywords

Even players who have read the rules twice still trip over the same handful of terms. Here are the five most common, based on community reports from beginner-friendly events.

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Treating "activate" as "move and shoot" | You forget actions are limited and order matters | Read the action list on the card before committing |
| Ignoring cover | You shoot the nearest model and wonder why nothing dies | Count cover before choosing a target |
| Skipping line of sight | You measure range first, then argue about a wall | Sight first, range second, every time |
| Chasing kills | You wipe out a squad and still lose on objectives | Re-read the mission keywords at the start of every round |
| Memorizing instead of reading | You rely on a half-remembered rule from a video | Keep the rulebook open for your first several games |

The underlying lesson is simple: the Halo Flashpoint keywords are not decoration on the cards. They are the rules. When a game goes sideways, it is almost always because someone skipped a keyword rather than because the dice betrayed them.

## FAQ

**Do I need to memorize every Halo Flashpoint keyword before my first game?**
No. Learn activation, actions, line of sight, cover, and objectives — those five cover the vast majority of decisions you'll make. The rest you can look up mid-game without slowing anyone down.

**What's the difference between a "unit" and a "model"?**
A model is a single miniature on the table. A unit is the group that activates together. When a keyword says "unit," it usually means every model in that group is affected, which is why the distinction matters during combat resolution.

**How important is line of sight, really?**
It is the gatekeeper of every ranged attack. If you get line of sight wrong, nothing downstream matters — your dice pool, your cover modifier, and your target choice are all built on a shot that may not have been legal.

**Where can I find official clarifications for unclear Halo Flashpoint keywords?**
Start with the publisher's official site and the rulebook's appendix, then check community FAQ documents maintained by tournament organizers. When in doubt, agree on an interpretation before the game starts and apply it consistently for both players.

Mastering the Halo Flashpoint keywords won't make you unbeatable, but it will make you fast, consistent, and pleasant to play against — and in a skirmish game where every activation counts, that's most of the battle won before the first die hits the table.
