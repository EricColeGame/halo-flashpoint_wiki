Halo: Flashpoint puts beautifully sculpted Spartans and Covenant warriors on your tabletop, and a little paint turns those bare plastic sprues into a force that looks like it marched straight out of the games. A solid Halo Flashpoint painting guide matters because these miniatures live or die on hard edges, glossy visors, and glowing energy weapons — details that reward planning far more than raw talent. This Halo Flashpoint painting guide walks you through priming, basecoats, edge highlighting, energy effects, and basing, so you can field a table-ready squad without repainting anything twice.

## Why Halo: Flashpoint Miniatures Reward a Plan

Halo: Flashpoint is a skirmish game, which changes the painting math completely. You are not batch-painting forty identical troopers; you are finishing a handful of elite operatives, so every shoulder pad and helmet becomes a focal point. Shortcuts that disappear in a ranked-up regiment stand out painfully on a five-model squad.

The sculpts also push you toward specific techniques. MJOLNIR armor is built from flat, angular plates with crisp panel lines — ideal surfaces for edge highlighting and panel-line washes. Covenant armor runs the other way: organic curves, layered plating, and exposed musculature that respond better to glazes, stippling, and metallic layering.

Finally, the game's visual identity is inseparable from its lighting. Plasma rifles, energy swords, and active camouflage are all *light sources*. Painting them convincingly is what separates a good squad from a great one.

### What to Gather Before Your First Brushstroke

| Item | Why It Matters | Budget Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Primer (black + light gray or white) | Establishes your value range before any color goes down | Any matte rattlecan primer designed for plastic |
| Three to five base colors | Covers armor, cloth, and weapons without decision fatigue | A single starter paint set |
| One wash or contrast-style paint | Adds instant depth to panel lines and recesses | Thinned acrylic ink |
| Two highlight tones per main color | Creates the crisp, hard-lit look Halo armor demands | Mix white into your base color |
| Fine-tip brush (size 0 or 00) | Essential for edge highlights and visor reflections | Any synthetic detail brush |
| Matte varnish | Protects paint during play | Brush-on matte medium |

## The Five-Stage Workflow

Beginners usually lose time by jumping between stages. Work in order, and a full squad comes together in a few evenings.

| Stage | Goal | Typical Time per Squad | Key Tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Assembly and cleanup | Remove mold lines, fill gaps | 30–60 min | Hobby knife, sanding sticks |
| 2. Priming | Even, thin coverage | 15 min plus drying | Rattlecan or airbrush |
| 3. Basecoating | Block in every color | 60–90 min | Size 2 brush |
| 4. Shading and layering | Depth plus volume | 90–120 min | Wash, size 1 brush |
| 5. Details and finishing | Visors, glow, base, varnish | 60 min | Size 00 brush |

The single biggest time-saver is step 1. Mold lines that survive into the painting stage will catch every wash and highlight you apply, and they cannot be fixed afterward.

## Priming and Basecoating: Building the Foundation

### Zenithal Priming for Angular Armor

Zenithal priming — a dark primer followed by a light spray from directly above — is close to cheating on Halo: Flashpoint miniatures. Because the armor is composed of flat planes, the light-to-dark gradient tells you exactly where highlights belong before you pick up a brush.

Spray matte black from all angles, let it cure fully, then dust light gray or white from roughly 45 degrees above the model. Keep the light coat thin. If you lose the black in the deepest recesses, you have gone too far.

### Choosing a Palette You Can Actually Finish

Pick a limited palette: one dominant armor color, one secondary, one metallic, and one accent for glow. Everything else is a variation on those four.

| Armor Type | Basecoat | Shade | Highlight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classic green MJOLNIR | Dark olive green | Green-black wash | Olive plus pale yellow |
| Blue team armor | Deep ultramarine | Blue-black wash | Sky blue |
| Red team armor | Deep crimson | Dark red-brown wash | Bright scarlet |
| White or gray armor | Pale gray | Cool gray wash | Pure white |
| Elite/Banished plating | Dark purple or steel | Purple-black wash | Lilac or silver |

Write your recipes down. Nothing derails a project faster than forgetting which two colors you mixed for a shoulder pad three weeks ago.

## Painting MJOLNIR Armor Step by Step

The armor is the star of the model, and the technique is straightforward once you sequence it properly.

1. **Basecoat every plate** with your chosen armor color, thinned to a milk-like consistency. Two thin coats beat one thick coat every time.
2. **Wash the panel lines** with a dark, thinned wash. Let capillary action pull it into the recesses rather than flooding the whole surface.
3. **Re-establish the base color** on the raised planes, leaving the wash only in the recesses.
4. **Layer a mid-tone** on the upper two-thirds of each plate, following the zenithal light.
5. **Edge highlight** every hard corner with a fine-tip brush, dragging the side of the bristles along the edge rather than the tip.
6. **Spot highlight** the most prominent edges — helmet crown, shoulder rims, knee plates — with your brightest tone.

That final step is what makes armor read as metal-ceramic composite instead of painted plastic. Reserve the brightest highlight for fewer than 20 percent of the edges; restraint is what sells the effect.

### Painting Black Armor Without Losing Detail

Black armor is a common trap. Pure black plus black wash equals a featureless silhouette. Instead, basecoat with a very dark gray, shade with black, and highlight up through mid-gray to a light blue-gray. The eye will read it as black while the sculpt stays legible on the tabletop.

## Covenant and Banished Forces: A Different Approach

Where Spartans want crisp edges, Covenant models want organic transitions. Organic armor is curved and layered, so your highlights should follow the form in soft bands rather than sharp lines.

| Faction Element | Palette Direction | Best Technique | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elite armor plating | Purple, teal, or steel with metallic trim | Layering plus glazes | Hard edge highlights everywhere |
| Grunt skin and gear | Orange-tan skin, gas-mask tanks, backpacks | Stippling for mottled skin | Flat single-tone skin |
| Jackal shields and gear | Cool grays, teal accents, bright shield face | Glazing the translucent shield | Over-thinning the glaze |
| Brute armor | Rusty browns, bone, worn metal | Sponge chipping and drybrush | Clean, uniform finishes |
| Metallic weapons | Gunmetal with warm highlights | Drybrush plus selective wash | Heavy wash pooling |

Brutes and other battle-worn troops benefit enormously from chipping: tear a small piece of foam, dip it in dark brown or silver, and dab most of it off before touching the model. Focus chipping on the edges and lower legs, where damage naturally accumulates.

## Energy Weapons, Visors, and Glow Effects

Glowing elements are the payoff. The principle behind every glow effect is contrast: the light source must be the brightest thing on the model, and the surrounding surface must be noticeably darker.

| Element | Approach | Key Trick |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma weapon coils | Bright cyan or green over white | Thin glaze of the glow color on nearby armor |
| Energy sword blade | White core, saturated blue edges | Keep the core small and hot |
| Visor | Deep base, sharp reflected highlight | Gloss varnish for a glassy finish |
| Active camo | Cool blue-gray with soft blur | Blend transitions rather than hard edges |
| Forerunner tech | Warm orange or cool blue | Let the glow spill onto the base |

For a plasma rifle, paint the coils white first, then build up your glow color in thin glazes toward the edges. Finish with a very small pure-white highlight at the hottest point. Then take the glow color, thin it to a wash, and brush it lightly onto the armor directly facing the coil. That spill light is what makes the weapon look like it is actually emitting energy rather than simply being painted bright.

## Basing, Sealing, and Finishing Touches

Bases unify a squad visually and anchor the glow effects you just painted. Keep them simple and consistent across the whole force.

| Base Style | Materials | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Urban rubble | Sand, small cork chunks, gray drybrush | UNSC-focused forces |
| Alien jungle | Static grass, coarse flock, green-brown blend | Covenant or Banished squads |
| Forerunner metal | Textured plastic card, metallic drybrush, blue glow | Mixed forces |
| Snow or ice | Baking soda mix, cool blue-white drybrush | Any army, fast to paint |

Paint the base rim a clean, uniform color — black or a neutral brown — because it frames the miniature and makes the squad look intentional.

Finish with two thin coats of matte varnish. Miniatures get handled constantly during skirmish play, and varnish is the difference between a squad that lasts for years and one that needs touch-ups after a month. If you want glossy visors and energy blades, apply a gloss varnish selectively over those areas after the matte coat dries.

## Troubleshooting Common Problems

| Problem | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Paint looks chalky | Thick coats or dry brushing wet paint | Thin more, use a wet palette |
| Washes leave tide marks | Too much wash applied at once | Wick excess off with a dry brush |
| Highlights look messy | Brush too large or too wet | Switch to size 00, dry the tip |
| Details lost under paint | Too many layers on fine areas | Reprime the area and work thinner |
| Glow effect looks flat | No contrast around the light source | Darken surrounding armor, add spill light |

Community reports from tabletop painting forums consistently point to the same root cause: too much paint, applied too fast. Slower, thinner, and more layers almost always wins.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to paint a full Halo: Flashpoint squad?**

Most painters finish a five- to eight-model squad in six to ten hours spread across several sessions. Using a zenithal prime plus a wash-and-highlight approach on armor cuts that considerably, because you skip the slowest part of layering from a flat basecoat.

**Do I need an airbrush for a Halo Flashpoint painting guide to be worth following?**

No. An airbrush speeds up priming and smooth armor gradients, but every technique in this guide works with rattlecan primer and regular brushes. Zenithal priming from a can and careful edge highlighting will get you 90 percent of the way there.

**Should I paint miniatures before or after assembling them?**

For most Halo: Flashpoint models, assemble first and paint after, since the poses are designed for gaming and the armor plates are accessible. Sub-assembly is only worth it for models with arms or weapons crossed tightly over the chest.

**What is the biggest mistake beginners make with these miniatures?**

Painting black armor in flat black and skipping the final bright edge highlight. Those two habits flatten the sculpt and erase the hard, angular silhouette that makes MJOLNIR armor so distinctive on the tabletop.

Paint one test model from start to finish before committing to a whole squad. Ten minutes of testing saves hours of rework — and it is the single most useful habit any Halo Flashpoint painting guide can teach you.
